Plastic in the Pantry

An exploratory look at PlasticList's chemical testing data, with a stop at Stanford Dining.

Aayan Ahmed · 618 lab samples · 236 products · Bay Area, 2024

618 samples tested for phthalates and bisphenols
236 food and beverage products covered
~12,000 ng DEHP in an average Stanford dining-hall meal, across three samples
13% median difference between repeat samples of the same product

PlasticList tested food and drink bought around the Bay Area for chemicals that migrate out of packaging: phthalates such as DEHP and its common substitute DEHT, and bisphenols such as BPA and BPS. This analysis asks three questions of that data. Which chemicals actually showed up, and how often? Do processed foods carry more than less-processed ones? And how much does a single measurement tell you, given that the same product tested twice rarely gives the same number?

DEHP and DEHT were detected above the lab's quantification limit in a majority of samples, while most of the other measured chemicals were non-detects in 85–100% of them. Within this dataset, processed and fast foods tended to show higher DEHP measurements than less-processed groups. A Stanford dining-hall meal averaged about 12,000 ng of DEHP per serving across three samples.

The repeat samples are the least dramatic result and the most important one for reading everything else. Median difference between two samples of the same product was about 13%, and some products differed by 40% or more. A single number for a product is an estimate with real spread around it, not a fixed property of that product.

The full write-up carries the code, the figures, and the group-by-group comparisons, and is set up so any number in it can be traced back to the filtering that produced it.
